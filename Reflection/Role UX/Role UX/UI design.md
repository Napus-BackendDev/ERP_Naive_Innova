**1. Lessons learned.** 
Using AI changed my UX/UI workflow most during early layout exploration. Figma AI only gave a useful layout after I mapped out the real workflow: order, tag linking, packaging, formula adjustment, R&D QC, photo verification, and shipping. **What got better** was speed—I quickly compared layouts and chose an overall progress bar with expandable product cards, which would have taken too long by hand. **What got worse** was consistency and domain accuracy. Because AI screens look finished right away, mistakes are easy to miss. Without enough context about our client, Naive Innova (a pet care OEM factory), AI filled the prototype with photos of human cosmetics and mock products such as a stainless bolt, which have nothing to do with pet care. One item also showed "6/6 steps (100%)" while steps below still said "waiting," and the customer view had an editable tracking field. Scanning a tag even exposed the buyer's company name and job number, a privacy issue we caught late. AI flagged none of these. I learned that domain, edge-case, and privacy checks must start during design, not after coding, and teams must lock in which AI output is final before handoff.

**2. Where I stand.** 
AI won't replace UX/UI designers, though it is replacing purely visual screen production. My value lies in understanding user and business context, turning messy workflows into clear requirements, and judging whether a design is accurate, usable, and safe. Today I am a junior designer who uses AI well, but I need stronger user research and usability testing skills to stand out.

**3. My plan.** 
Next year, I want a UX/UI internship on a real product to build 2–3 case studies grounded in research and usability testing, not just final screens. After graduating, I plan to work 2–3 years in industry to build stronger research habits and developer collaboration. In five years, I hope to pursue a master's in HCI or UX, focusing on designing transparent, trustworthy, and user-safe AI products.
